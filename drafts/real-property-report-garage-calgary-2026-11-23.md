# Real Property Report for a Garage: What Calgary Expects

The City of Calgary needs a site plan that shows where a new garage sits on your lot, and the City's own checklist offers a shortcut: use your real property report. That one document answers much of what a reviewer wants to see, and it shows early whether the garage will run into the location problem the City names most often.

This page explains how a real property report garage application works in Calgary, what the City's certificate of compliance does and does not check, and what to confirm before a builder starts drawing. If you would rather talk it through, [call (403) 555-0162](tel:+14035550162). Independent Calgary garage builders quote and contract with you directly, and the person doing the work is the one who applies for the permit.

City of Calgary web pages state that they have no legal status and cannot be used as an official interpretation of the bylaws. Treat everything below as a guide and confirm with the City before you commit to a design.

## What a real property report is, and why the City mentions it

A real property report, usually shortened to RPR, is a survey-based drawing of your lot prepared by an Alberta land surveyor. It shows the property lines and the location of the buildings on the lot. For a garage application that is exactly the information the City wants.

The City's Garage Permit Checklist says so plainly: "You can use a real property report as your site plan." The same line appears on the City's garage permit page. You are not required to hand over an RPR in place of a drawing, but if you have a clear one, it saves a builder from producing a site plan from scratch.

The word that matters is "clear". The City's certificate of compliance page says altered real property reports, unclear plans and plot plans will not be accepted. That line is on the compliance page, but it is a fair test for a site plan too: if your RPR has been marked up by hand or patched together from two documents, ask the builder to work from a clean copy.

## What the garage site plan has to show

The City's checklist lists what the site plan must contain. An RPR covers some of these items on its own, and the rest get added to it or drawn alongside it. Going through the list with your RPR in hand shows quickly what is missing.

- The address of the property.
- The property lines, and the streets and back alley or lane that border the lot.
- The outline of the garage, the house and any other buildings on the lot.
- The dimensions of the house and the garage.
- The distance from the property lines and from the house to the proposed garage.
- Windows and doors.
- The direction the roof trusses run.
- The length and location of the driveway or apron.
- The height from the parking surface to where the wall meets the roof, and from the parking surface to the peak.
- The exterior finish.

An existing RPR will usually show the lot lines and the house. It will not show a garage you have not built yet, and it will not show truss direction, door locations or heights. Those details come from the builder's drawings. If the garage is going where an old one stands, the RPR may show the old garage, so the plan has to make clear which building is staying and which is coming down.

The checklist adds that drawings stamped "not for construction", "preliminary" or "for permit purposes only" are not acceptable. If a registered right-of-way is on your title, the City also wants the Maintenance Access Right-of-Way documents. The City says permanent structures are not permitted in utility rights-of-way, and that includes footings, walls and eaves. Check your RPR and your title for one.

## Where the garage sits: the numbers the plan gets checked against

A reviewer reads the site plan against the Land Use Bylaw. These are the measurements to check on your RPR before anything else, using the bylaw text as the City publishes it.

- Side and rear property lines: a minimum of 0.6 metres in most cases, or 1.2 metres from a side or rear line shared with a street.
- The 0.6 metre setback can be reduced to zero in specific cases: a building under 10 square metres, a wall built of maintenance-free materials with no eave overhang onto the neighbour, or a registered private maintenance easement from the neighbour.
- Distance from the house: a minimum of 1.0 metre between any façade of a garage and the house. The City notes this includes second floor cantilevers and bay windows.
- Front: an accessory building must not sit in the actual front setback area.
- Lane access: the garage apron must be 0.6 metres in length, measured from the property line shared with the lane to the garage door.

Those are the same rules the permit post covers in general. For the wider process, including fees and timelines, see [the Calgary garage permit guide](/blog/calgary-garage-permit/). What an RPR adds is the evidence: it is the measured proof of where your house and property lines actually are.

## The most common refusal reason

When the City describes why a certificate of compliance gets refused, it names one reason as common: "Accessory Residential Buildings (such as Gazebos, sheds, garages) too close to the house."

That lines up with the 1.0 metre rule above. If a building of 10 square metres or more is closer than 1.0 metre to the house, the City says you will need a development permit. A development permit is a separate planning approval. The City lists its fee at $550 for proposed structures that do not meet all the rules, with "no guarantee of approval", and a stated timeline of 10 to 12 weeks. A building permit alone is listed at approximately 7 days. The City says those timelines are not a guarantee.

The practical point: measure the gap between the new garage wall and the house wall before the design is final. The City says the 1.0 metre rule includes second floor cantilevers and bay windows, so measure from the furthest-projecting part, not the wall behind it. A garage drawn at 0.9 metres from the house is a different application from one drawn at 1.0 metre, and the difference is in weeks and in the fee.

## The certificate of compliance: what it is and what it skips

A certificate of compliance is a City service that confirms the building locations on your lot comply with the Land Use Bylaw. The City says the confirmation is made on a real property report prepared by an Alberta land surveyor. The published numbers:

- Cost: $189 per parcel for single-detached, semi-detached and duplex properties.
- Initial review: 1 to 2 days.

It is a check of where buildings sit. The City lists several things a compliance does not review:

- Parcel coverage.
- Building height.
- Accessory residential buildings under 10 square metres, such as small sheds and pergolas.

That list matters for a garage. Passing a compliance check on the buildings already on your lot tells you nothing about whether a bigger garage would push the lot over its coverage limit, or whether a tall garage meets the height rule. The bylaw limits height to 4.6 metres and 3.0 metres at any eaveline, both measured from the finished floor, and to one storey. Parcel coverage depends on your district, and all accessory buildings together must not exceed the lesser of the house's building coverage or 75 square metres. Those checks are separate from the certificate, and they are part of the permit review.

So the certificate is most useful when the question is about existing structures, for example whether an old shed that will stay is a problem. For a new garage, the permit review is where the proposed building is tested.

## If you are building outside Calgary

Requirements change at the city limit. Airdrie's detached garage page says an application needs a real property report showing the proposed garage and setbacks, plus a building plan. That is stricter than Calgary's "you can use" wording, because Airdrie asks for the proposed garage to be on the report. Airdrie also says starting work before the permit may result in permit fees being doubled. See [garage builders in Airdrie](/garage-builders-airdrie/) for the local picture.

Other towns publish their own requirements, and Rocky View County, Cochrane, Okotoks and Chestermere each run separate permit processes. Check the town's page before you order a survey or a drawing.

## Who applies, and what the fee is

The City says that if you hire someone to do the work, they need to apply for the permit. In practice that means the garage builder you choose files the application, using your RPR or a site plan drawn from it. The City's 2026 building permit fee for a new garage is $321, plus a 4 per cent Safety Codes Council fee, for a total of $333.84. A separate electrical permit is always needed for a new garage, for the lights and the overhead door connection.

Builders differ in what they include. Venkor Group says City permits and design drawings are included in its quote. ## Before you ask for quotes

A short list to run through with your RPR on the table:

- Check that it is legible and unaltered.
- Measure the gap between the house and the planned garage wall, including any cantilever or bay window.
- Look for registered rights-of-way or easements marked on the lot.
- Note where the lane meets your property line, because the 0.6 metre apron is measured from there.
- Write down the lot's district, since coverage limits depend on it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a real property report to get a garage permit in Calgary?

The City says you can use an RPR as your site plan, which makes it the easiest route but not the only one. A site plan with all the checklist items can also be submitted. In Airdrie, the application needs an RPR showing the proposed garage.

### How much does a certificate of compliance cost in Calgary?

The City lists $189 per parcel for single-detached, semi-detached and duplex properties, with an initial review of 1 to 2 days. It is prepared against a real property report from an Alberta land surveyor.

### What does a certificate of compliance not check?

The City says it does not review parcel coverage, building height, or accessory buildings under 10 square metres. A new garage still has to meet those rules in the permit review.

### Will the City accept a marked-up real property report?

Not for a certificate of compliance. The City says altered real property reports, unclear plans and plot plans will not be accepted there. Use a clean copy, and let the builder add the garage details on a separate drawing.
